# Navy primary-care physician retention — recommendation

**Recommendation.** Test a four-year, specialty-targeted retention-bonus schedule for eligible Navy primary-care physicians in San Diego, calibrated to close half of each specialty’s modeled current cash gap. The provisional **total annual** bonuses are about **$55,000 for pediatrics, $139,000 for general internal medicine, and $96,000 for family medicine**, subject to verifying comparable civilian-only offers, physician eligibility, budget, and legal authority. Track acceptance at the first unobligated decision and later retention against a comparable group, while recording assignment changes and opportunities to practice the primary specialty.

## Why

First, civilian practice is the **opportunity cost** of staying in the Navy. Raising the bonus changes the financial **incentive** at the service-agreement decision. The published Marit San Diego benchmarks imply Year 1 cash gaps under current bonuses of about **$40,008 for pediatrics, $182,091 for internal medicine, and $96,072 for family medicine**. A specialty schedule responds to those different outside options rather than assuming one amount makes every physician equally competitive. The [analysis table](../../analysis/physician-retention-analysis.md) and [figure](../../figures/physician-retention-cumulative-gap.svg) show the calculation and its four-year path.

Second, the flat $100,000 total bonus is a less precise use of funds under these assumptions. It would put modeled pediatric Navy cash about **$24,992 above** the published benchmark in Year 1, while leaving an internal-medicine gap of about **$130,091** and a family-medicine gap of about **$44,072**. This is **marginal analysis**: compare the extra bonus dollar with the remaining financial gap and, once retention data exist, with the cost per additional physician retained. The proposed 50% schedule adds about **$20,004, $91,046, and $48,036 per physician per year** to the current bonuses, respectively. It is an illustrative dose, not a research-derived threshold.

Third, the staffing decision is current. [GAO's staffing review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106988) reports that assigned military medical personnel across DOD fell about 16% from FY2015 to FY2023 and that substantial shortfalls were expected through at least 2027. That figure is not a Navy physician retention rate. [GAO's incentives review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-165) also reports that DOD lacks consistent replacement-cost, bonus-acceptance, and civilian-wage data needed to judge incentive effectiveness. A measured pilot would collect precisely those inputs.

## The judgment call

Pay is not the only reason a physician stays. Predictable assignments and protected clinical practice may reduce the **compensating wage differential** required for Navy service. Public [Medical Corps community values](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/fy26_ac_staff_community_briefs.pdf) emphasize specialty practice and professional growth, while a [2026 GAO review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107677) describes clinical-readiness opportunities from civilian partnerships and gaps in activity data. Neither source quantifies a retention effect. Pension, healthcare, and GI Bill rights affect **present value** only when staying creates value beyond benefits already earned. The fixed cash table leaves those person-specific benefits unpriced. It also holds rank at O-4; a real promotion would change the pay comparison. The Marit benchmarks are self-reported, citywide, and mixed-employer; the especially high internist figure includes unlike career stages and a military-contractor entry in its public preview. The [Doximity national sensitivity check](../../analysis/physician-retention-analysis.md#national-sensitivity-check-separate-comparator) produces much smaller gaps. I would therefore treat the amounts above as planning figures and validate them with actual comparable local offers before committing a program-wide schedule.

## What would change my answer

I would revise the pay-first approach if a meaningful targeted bonus increase failed to improve acceptance and subsequent retention relative to a comparable group, or if assignment predictability retained more eligible physicians per dollar. I would also lower or redirect the proposed amounts if verified civilian-only San Diego offers showed materially smaller specialty gaps. Until those outcomes are observed, the model describes cash competitiveness and marginal bonus cost; it does not estimate the **elasticity of labor supply to the Navy** or prove how many physicians the policy would retain.

## Implementation and compensation caveat

FY26 [NAVADMIN 229/25](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/NAVADMIN%20229-25.pdf?ver=TfDkIMOG-xZUcffS8qWIAw%3D%3D) continues existing special-pay authority and directs officers to [corps-specific guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Special-Pays/). The proposed amounts are a **policy test**, not current authorized bonuses. I would verify Medical Corps eligibility, agreement terms, statutory and policy ceilings, funding, and approval authority before launch. Medical Service Corps pay guidance does not govern physician rates. [37 U.S.C. § 335(e)(1)(C)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A37+section%3A335+edition%3Aprelim%29) sets a $150,000 annual ceiling for the relevant retention-bonus authority; the modeled internal-medicine amount is below that ceiling, but above the current FY26 Navy schedule. The ceiling permits analysis of a change; it does not approve one.

The pay gap also needs a total-compensation check before the dollar schedule is set. Navy cash already includes BAH and BAS, which [DoD identifies as generally tax-exempt](https://militarypay.defense.gov/Pay/Tax-Information/Exempt/). An individual tax calculation could narrow the after-tax gap. Pension and BRS/TSP, TRICARE, and potential GI Bill transfer could add value **only to the extent staying changes what the physician receives**; already earned rights should not be credited again. Leave, clinical development, assignment stability, and mission value should be recorded alongside pay. A civilian offer may include its own retirement, health, leave, and malpractice benefits. I would use verified offers and physician-specific benefit inputs to refine the bonus amounts while retaining the specialty-targeted test and its measured retention outcome.

For evaluation, I would seek the annual provider-retention survey required by [Section 718 of Public Law 118-159](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ159/pdf/PLAW-118publ159.pdf), alongside administrative records of bonus eligibility, acceptance, and eventual separation. The survey can reveal stated reasons for staying or leaving; the administrative records are needed to test whether the bonus changes behavior.

**Source note.** Preliminary internal survey findings signal retention concern, but they measure stated intentions rather than observed separations. Because the underlying briefing is controlled and has not been verified as publicly releasable, this public memo omits its figures. Obtain an approved aggregate release and match it to administrative retention outcomes before using those numbers as evidence in the paper.

**Pay-plan baseline.** The corrected [FY25 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY25/FY25%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE-Correction.pdf?ver=Fu3zLzlHCUzEu0ynrllaAQ%3D%3D) set the four-year general internal-medicine RB at $40,000 annually; [FY26 guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) raised it to $48,000. Pediatrics remained $35,000 and family medicine $48,000. The modeled baseline is therefore a new FY26-rate agreement, not a claim about every physician's existing contract. The evaluation should record agreement start year and actual RB/IP payments, and separate any effect of this existing $8,000 internal-medicine increase from the proposed additional pilot bonus.

**Next sensitivity.** [FY26 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) also lists six-year bonuses above the four-year schedule for these specialties. I would compare that option only across a full six-year horizon, including the extra two years of obligation and the post-agreement retention outcome. For individual pension value, I would distinguish 20-year eligibility from student or training time credited in the retired-pay calculation, as described in the [DoD USU credit memorandum](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/usuhs-retirement-credit.pdf), and verify the member's Statement of Service. Neither refinement changes the current four-year gross-cash arithmetic.
