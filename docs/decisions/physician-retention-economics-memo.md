# Navy primary-care physician retention — economics memo

**Decision to test.** I would test a larger retention bonus for eligible Navy pediatricians, general internists, and family physicians in San Diego, comparing it with a more predictable assignment program. My working hypothesis is that increased pay will improve retention, but the amount needed and the size of the response remain unmeasured.

## Why pay could matter

The **opportunity cost** of remaining in the Navy is the civilian job and other opportunities a physician gives up. That outside option varies by specialty and household circumstances. A retention bonus changes the **incentive** at the point when a physician can choose whether to sign another service agreement. Assignment stability, clinical opportunities, and the burden of moving also affect that choice. In labor economics, a physician may require a **compensating wage differential** for less control over location or practice; conversely, service-related benefits may offset some of the cash gap.

The relevant comparison is **total incremental compensation**, not base salary alone. Navy cash includes basic pay, housing and subsistence allowances, specialty pay, and the current bonus. Pension eligibility, healthcare, and GI Bill transfer rights should enter only to the extent that staying creates additional value compared with leaving at the same decision date. An already-earned benefit is not a new retention incentive. The timing of a possible pension also makes **present value** important: a future payment is worth less than the same payment today, and a physician who may not reach 20 years should not be credited with a certain lifetime pension.

## What the lab measures

The [interactive pay-gap lab](../../capabilities/economic-research/physician-pay-lab.html) applies **marginal analysis** to ask how much *additional annual bonus* closes a selected physician's modeled financial gap. Under its default O-4, over-10-years, with-dependents, ZIP 92134 internal-medicine scenario, current Navy cash is about $276,966 and the published Marit San Diego benchmark is $459,057. The resulting cash gap is about $182,091 per year; closing it would require a total annual retention bonus of about $230,091, including the current $48,000 bonus. A proposed $100,000 total bonus narrows that gap but does not close it. These are arithmetic scenarios, not recommended or necessarily authorized bonus amounts. Marit's benchmark is citywide, self-reported, and not a verified civilian-only offer.

The same bonus can have different effects across specialties because each physician faces a different outside option. That is why a flat $100,000 policy and a specialty-targeted policy should be compared on both added cost and remaining gap. The Navy's **marginal cost** is the additional bonus paid per eligible physician; the desired marginal benefit is an additional physician retained and the readiness and replacement costs thereby avoided. The lab calculates the cost and financial gap, but it cannot yet calculate cost per additional physician retained.

## What would test the hypothesis

The empirical question is the **elasticity of labor supply to the Navy**: how much eligible physicians' decision to stay changes when their compensation rises. A smaller modeled pay gap does not itself measure that response. I would compare retention before and after a targeted bonus increase with a suitable comparison group, while recording specialty, years of service, eligibility, assignment changes, and civilian opportunities. I would also compare retention gained per dollar with an assignment-predictability pilot. If bonuses materially narrow the gap but retention is unchanged, or if assignment reform retains more physicians per dollar, I would revise the pay-first recommendation.

**Sources and limits.** Pay and bonus inputs are documented in the [model specification](../../capabilities/economic-research/spec.md) using [DFAS](https://www.dfas.mil/MilitaryMembers/payentitlements/Pay-Tables/Basic-Pay/CO/), [Navy Medicine](https://www.med.navy.mil/Special-Pays/), [DTMO](https://www.travel.dod.mil/Allowances/Basic-Allowance-for-Housing/BAH-Rate-Lookup/), and [Marit Health](https://www.marithealth.com/o/-/internist/salary/san-diego-ca). [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-165) identifies gaps in the data needed to evaluate incentive effectiveness. The pay-gap lab is a sensitivity tool; it does not prove a causal retention effect.
