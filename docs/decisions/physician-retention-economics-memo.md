# Navy primary-care physician retention — recommendation

**Recommendation.** Estimate the **financial opportunity cost of staying** for eligible San Diego-based Navy pediatricians, general internists, and family physicians at their next unobligated decision. Compare the expected present value of a feasible civilian path with continued Navy service over the same horizon, using after-tax cash, benefits that actually change with the choice, physician-paid expenses, and service obligations. Report a range where inputs are uncertain. Then test specialty-targeted bonuses against that range, measuring agreement acceptance and later retention. The $100,000 annual total bonus in the committed research brief remains the original hypothesis and a comparison scenario; closing 50% of a gross salary gap remains a sensitivity case, not the recommended amount.

## Why this is the decision metric

The civilian alternative is the physician's **opportunity cost** of another Navy agreement. The published Marit San Diego figures imply Year 1 **gross cash** gaps under current bonuses of about **$40,008 for pediatrics, $182,091 for internal medicine, and $96,072 for family medicine**. These are useful starting observations, but the salary figures are citywide and mixed-employer. Gross cash also leaves out differential taxes, civilian employer retirement and health benefits, pension continuation value, GI Bill transfer, and expenses the physician would personally pay. The [analysis table](../../analysis/physician-retention-analysis.md) and [figure](../../figures/physician-retention-cumulative-gap.svg) remain the transparent cash baseline.

| Specialty | Current Year 1 cash gap | Total annual RB needed for gross cash parity | Gap with a $100,000 total RB |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Pediatrics | $40,008 | $75,008 | Navy cash exceeds benchmark by $24,992 |
| Internal medicine | $182,091 | $230,091 | $130,091 remains |
| Family medicine | $96,072 | $144,072 | $44,072 remains |

These are arithmetic comparisons, **not** recommended bonus rates. Discounting the modeled four-year gross-cash gaps at an illustrative 5% real rate gives present values of about **$139,000 for pediatrics, $668,000 for internal medicine, and $348,000 for family medicine** (2027 as year 0). Those are cash-only proxies; individual taxes, benefits, expenses, and actual offers could change them substantially. In particular, the internal-medicine gross-cash parity figure exceeds the $150,000 annual ceiling discussed below. I would not call any of these values an exact reservation wage or a prediction of retention.

The model should show (1) the present-value financial difference between the two paths, (2) its annual equivalent over expected additional service, and (3) the **nonfinancial value the physician would need to place on Navy service** for staying to be preferable at a given bonus. Assignment stability, protected clinical practice, family time, and mission matter, but their dollar values are not observed in salary data. Survey or choice data could estimate how preferences vary; absent those data, the nonfinancial figure is a required threshold, not an asserted benefit.

The staffing decision is current. [GAO's staffing review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106988) reports that assigned military medical personnel across DOD fell about 16% from FY2015 to FY2023 and that substantial shortfalls were expected through at least 2027. That is not a Navy primary-care retention rate. [GAO's incentives review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-165) reports that DOD lacks consistent replacement-cost, bonus-acceptance, and civilian-wage data needed to judge incentive effectiveness. A measured pilot should collect those inputs and compare marginal bonus cost per additional physician retained with avoidable replacement and readiness costs.

## The judgment call

Pay is not the only reason a physician stays. Predictable assignments and protected clinical practice may reduce the **compensating wage differential** required for Navy service. Public [Medical Corps community values](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/fy26_ac_staff_community_briefs.pdf) emphasize specialty practice and professional growth, while a [2026 GAO review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107677) describes clinical-readiness opportunities from civilian partnerships and gaps in activity data. Neither source quantifies a retention effect. Pension, healthcare, and GI Bill rights affect **present value** only when staying creates value beyond benefits already earned. The fixed cash table leaves those person-specific benefits unpriced. It also holds rank at O-4; a real promotion would change the pay comparison. The Marit benchmarks are self-reported, citywide, and mixed-employer; the especially high internist figure includes unlike career stages and a military-contractor entry in its public preview. The [Doximity national sensitivity check](../../analysis/physician-retention-analysis.md#national-sensitivity-check-separate-comparator) produces much smaller gaps. I would therefore treat the cash results as planning figures, validate them with comparable local offers, and estimate a range for the person-specific financial opportunity cost before setting any program-wide schedule.

## How the course economics changes the decision

The lectures make the 50% schedule a **testable policy dose**, not a predicted break-even point. BUS 620 Session 1's elasticity lesson (pp. 18, 24–30) requires an observed change in retention after a compensation change; the oil responses in that lecture cannot stand in for a physician response. Micro C6's consumer-choice and indifference-curve lessons (consumer choice pp. 5–14; reading pp. 3–9) also mean that cash, assignment stability, clinical practice, and family time are a bundle. Equal gross cash does not prove that a physician is indifferent between jobs.

Micro C4's marginal-cost and sunk-cost distinction (pp. 7–15) changes the budget test: count **new** bonus spending and avoidable future costs, not HPSP or training money already spent. The outcome is additional physicians retained because of the policy, measured at the first unobligated decision and again after the agreement. The “Who Paid?” lecture (BUS 620 Session 3, pp. 35–43) further requires separating the Navy's fiscal expense and the physician's payment from any measured patient-access or readiness gain. A bonus paid to someone who would have stayed is not an extra retained physician. These concepts support a targeted pilot with an explicit comparison group and cost-per-additional-retention measure; they do not establish that 50% is the optimal gap share.

## What would change my answer

I would revise the pay-first approach if a meaningful targeted bonus increase failed to improve acceptance and subsequent retention relative to a comparable group, or if assignment predictability retained more eligible physicians per dollar. I would also revise the estimated opportunity-cost range if verified civilian-only San Diego offers, taxes, benefits, or physician-paid expenses materially changed the comparison. Until those outcomes are observed, the model describes cash competitiveness and marginal bonus cost; it does not estimate the **elasticity of labor supply to the Navy** or prove how many physicians the policy would retain.

## Implementation and compensation caveat

FY26 [NAVADMIN 229/25](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/NAVADMIN%20229-25.pdf?ver=TfDkIMOG-xZUcffS8qWIAw%3D%3D) continues existing special-pay authority and directs officers to [corps-specific guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Special-Pays/). Any pilot rate would require approval; the modeled amounts are **analytical scenarios**, not currently authorized bonuses. I would verify Medical Corps eligibility, agreement terms, statutory and policy ceilings, funding, and approval authority before launch. Medical Service Corps pay guidance does not govern physician rates. [37 U.S.C. § 335(e)(1)(C)](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A37+section%3A335+edition%3Aprelim%29) sets a $150,000 annual ceiling for the relevant retention-bonus authority. The $139,046 internal-medicine half-gap scenario is below that ceiling, while its $230,091 gross-cash-parity figure is above it. A statutory ceiling does not approve a particular rate.

The opportunity-cost estimate needs a total-compensation check before a dollar schedule is set. Navy cash already includes BAH and BAS, which [DoD identifies as generally tax-exempt](https://militarypay.defense.gov/Pay/Tax-Information/Exempt/). An individual tax calculation could narrow the after-tax gap. Pension and BRS/TSP, TRICARE, and potential GI Bill transfer could add value **only to the extent staying changes what the physician receives**; already earned rights should not be credited again. Leave, clinical development, assignment stability, and mission value should be recorded alongside pay. A civilian offer may include its own retirement, health, leave, and malpractice benefits. I would use verified offers and physician-specific benefit inputs to estimate a range, then select test doses that are feasible under pay authority and large enough to distinguish a retention response.

For evaluation, I would seek the annual provider-retention survey required by [Section 718 of Public Law 118-159](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ159/pdf/PLAW-118publ159.pdf), alongside administrative records of bonus eligibility, acceptance, and eventual separation. The survey can reveal stated reasons for staying or leaving; the administrative records are needed to test whether the bonus changes behavior.

**Source note.** Preliminary internal survey findings signal retention concern, but they measure stated intentions rather than observed separations. Because the underlying briefing is controlled and has not been verified as publicly releasable, this public memo omits its figures. Obtain an approved aggregate release and match it to administrative retention outcomes before using those numbers as evidence in the paper.

**Pay-plan baseline.** The corrected [FY25 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY25/FY25%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE-Correction.pdf?ver=Fu3zLzlHCUzEu0ynrllaAQ%3D%3D) set the four-year general internal-medicine RB at $40,000 annually; [FY26 guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) raised it to $48,000. Pediatrics remained $35,000 and family medicine $48,000. The modeled baseline is therefore a new FY26-rate agreement, not a claim about every physician's existing contract. The evaluation should record agreement start year and actual RB/IP payments, and separate any effect of this existing $8,000 internal-medicine increase from the proposed additional pilot bonus.

**Next sensitivity.** [FY26 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) also lists six-year bonuses above the four-year schedule for these specialties. I would compare that option only across a full six-year horizon, including the extra two years of obligation and the post-agreement retention outcome. For individual pension value, I would distinguish 20-year eligibility from student or training time credited in the retired-pay calculation, as described in the [DoD USU credit memorandum](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/usuhs-retirement-credit.pdf), and verify the member's Statement of Service. Neither refinement changes the current four-year gross-cash arithmetic.
