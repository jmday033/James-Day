# Navy primary-care physician retention — decision memo

**Recommendation.** Quantify the financial opportunity cost of another Navy agreement for San Diego-based pediatricians, general internists, and family physicians under current pay. Test whether targeted, accurate communication about available Navy-specific nonfinancial opportunities improves agreement acceptance and later retention, while measuring whether physicians can actually access those opportunities. Where feasible, compare communication with a real change in assignment, clinical, or professional-development access. This communication strategy is the working hypothesis; its effect is unmeasured. Use the financial estimate to identify where a large civilian advantage may limit what information alone can accomplish.

**Why the local case has global stakes.** [Naval Medical Forces Pacific](https://www.med.navy.mil/Naval-Medical-Forces-Pacific/Region/) is headquartered in San Diego and includes commands in Hawaii, Japan, and Guam. Its [mission](https://www.med.navy.mil/Naval-Medical-Forces-Pacific/Region/Mission/) is to keep assigned medical forces staffed, trained, equipped, and ready to deploy. A San Diego physician's civilian alternative is therefore relevant to a workforce that supports Pacific operations. This analysis does not establish that departures in pediatrics, internal medicine, or family medicine have reduced readiness. That claim needs specialty-specific vacancies, billet requirements, clinical capacity, deployability, and assignment data.

## Working hypothesis

At current pay rates, informing eligible physicians about real opportunities for specialty practice, leadership, teaching, research, operational work, professional development, and service may increase retention among those who value these attributes. Pension, healthcare, GI Bill rights, and existing bonuses remain part of the financial comparison. The hypothesis is about better awareness of **nonfinancial** opportunities, not a claim that messages can replace a missing opportunity or erase a pay gap.

## Why this is the decision metric

The civilian alternative is the physician's opportunity cost of another Navy agreement. The published Marit San Diego figures imply Year 1 gross cash gaps under current bonuses of about $40,008 for pediatrics, $182,091 for internal medicine, and $96,072 for family medicine. These are useful starting observations, but the salary figures are citywide and mixed-employer. Gross cash also leaves out differential taxes, civilian employer retirement and health benefits, pension continuation value, GI Bill transfer, and expenses the physician would personally pay. The [analysis table](physician-retention-analysis.md) and [figure](physician-retention-cumulative-gap.svg) remain the transparent cash baseline.

| Specialty | Year 1 current cash gap | Four-year current cash gap | PV at 5% |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Pediatrics | $40,008 | $148,794 | $139,009 |
| Internal medicine | $182,091 | $717,126 | $668,019 |
| Family medicine | $96,072 | $373,050 | $347,749 |

These are cash-only benchmark comparisons, not exact opportunity costs or predictions of retention. Individual taxes, benefits, expenses, and actual offers could change them substantially.

The model should show (1) the present-value financial difference between the two paths, (2) its annual equivalent over expected additional service, and (3) the **nonfinancial value the physician would need to place on Navy service** for staying to be preferable under the current package. Assignment stability, protected clinical practice, family time, and mission matter, but their dollar values are not observed in salary data. Survey or choice data could estimate how preferences vary; absent those data, the nonfinancial figure is a required threshold, not an asserted benefit.

The interactive lab lets a physician compare a longer active-duty path with rising civilian earnings and, optionally, a civilian-plus-Reserve path. For example, someone at 10 active years can enter 10 additional years to reach active-duty retirement, then compare its pension with a possible Reserve pension based on qualifying years, retirement points, and pay-start age. The [DoD active](https://militarypay.defense.gov/Pay/Retirement/ActiveDuty/) and [Reserve](https://militarypay.defense.gov/Pay/Retirement/Reserve/) rules distinguish eligibility and pension formulas; future Navy TSP contributions are entered separately. This sensitivity assumes completion of the chosen paths and does not assign a retention probability. It also does not model promotion, Reserve drill earnings, or different earnings after military retirement. The four-year Marit table above remains a cash-only starting point.

The staffing decision is current. [GAO's staffing review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-106988) reports that assigned military medical personnel across DOD fell about 16% from FY2015 to FY2023 and that substantial shortfalls were expected through at least 2027. That is not a Navy primary-care retention rate. [GAO's incentives review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-165) reports that DOD lacks consistent replacement-cost, bonus-acceptance, and civilian-wage data needed to judge incentive effectiveness. The next step is to collect those inputs and evaluate the existing FY26 pay change alongside other retention factors.

The decision has two empirical steps. First, test whether accurate information changes acceptance and later retention relative to standard information. Second, establish whether any additional retained physicians fill Pacific-relevant billets or preserve deployable clinical capacity. Count a staffing or readiness benefit only when the corresponding outcome is observed; a modeled salary gap cannot supply that evidence.

## The judgment call

Pay is not the only reason a physician stays. Predictable assignments and protected clinical practice may reduce the **compensating wage differential** required for Navy service. Public [Medical Corps community values](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/fy26_ac_staff_community_briefs.pdf) emphasize specialty practice and professional growth, while a [2026 GAO review](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107677) describes clinical-readiness opportunities from civilian partnerships and gaps in activity data. Neither source quantifies a retention effect. Pension, healthcare, and GI Bill rights affect **present value** only when staying creates value beyond benefits already earned. The fixed cash table leaves those person-specific benefits unpriced. It also holds rank at O-4; a real promotion would change the pay comparison. The Marit benchmarks are self-reported, citywide, and mixed-employer; the especially high internist figure includes unlike career stages and a military-contractor entry in its public preview. The [Doximity and SalaryDr national sensitivity checks](physician-retention-analysis.md#national-sensitivity-check-separate-comparator) show much smaller internist gaps, although SalaryDr's internist median includes hospitalists. I would therefore treat the cash results as planning figures, validate them with comparable local offers, and estimate a range for the person-specific financial opportunity cost before setting any program-wide schedule.

[White Coat Investor's 2012 stay-or-separate essay](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/should-i-stay-or-should-i-go-financial-implications-of-military-separation/) reinforces the need to compare career paths rather than one year's salary: retirement prospects, tax-adjusted pay, the ability to use civilian earnings sooner, and the location or deployment demands of service all affect the choice. Its personal account also names leadership, distinctive clinical work, and service as possible reasons to stay, which helps define what an accurate information campaign could describe. The 2012 annuity quote, taxes, and legacy pension example are not suitable FY26 values. Our four-year O-4 scenario does not reach 20 years, so its $0 pension entry cannot be read as a finding that four more years of service create no retirement option value. I would add a separate career-path sensitivity to retirement using the physician's actual system and service credit before making that claim.

The [2019 White Coat Investor interview with Joel Schofer](https://www.whitecoatinvestor.com/being-a-military-doctor-podcast-93/) gives the communication proposal a concrete challenge: MCCareer.org already gathered scattered career information and job announcements. A new briefing must improve on what eligible physicians already know, with current and specialty-specific opportunities they can actually pursue. The interview also describes leadership and training alongside assignment uncertainty and clinical constraints. I would disclose both sides in the message and measure baseline awareness, rather than advertise only favorable examples. Schofer's emergency-medicine experience and informal satisfaction impressions do not establish what today's Navy primary-care physicians value or how their retention responds.

The peer-reviewed evidence favors testing the substance of those opportunities, not just their description. In an [Army study that linked survey responses to subsequent voluntary separations](https://academic.oup.com/milmed/article/189/Supplement_3/39/7735915), family well-being, predictability, and duty-station choice were associated with leaving. A separate [military family-physician survey](https://academic.oup.com/milmed/article/189/7-8/e1414/7688228) found that perceived fair compensation was not significantly different from civilian survey responses even though compensation differed; its 55% stated-or-past post-obligation retention figure is not a Navy outcome rate. [Mentoring](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31665450/), [administrative support and rank](https://academic.oup.com/milmed/article-abstract/185/7-8/e1200/5814676), and [team coordination](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35050374/) also appear in observational military studies. These findings support measuring actual access, family and assignment conditions, and career stage. They do not show that a briefing causes retention.

The added literature rules out treating communication as the established best policy. A [transnational qualitative study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/10497323241254253) calls for tailored combinations of support, education, pay, and workload changes. A [review of reviews](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-025-12966-5) finds that turnover intentions are distinct from actual departures and that intervention evidence is context-dependent. [State support-for-service data](https://doi.org/10.1097/01.mlr.0000128003.81622.ef) and a [2026 rural primary-care review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-026-10218-8) make financial incentives a plausible alternative, though neither supplies a Navy bonus level. A new [military postgraduate-education survey](https://academic.oup.com/milmed/advance-article-abstract/doi/10.1093/milmed/usag408/8787182) links education with stated likelihood of staying, not observed retention. The policy choice is therefore empirical: measure current pay and real opportunities, then compare changes in actual decisions and separations. The [analysis evidence table](physician-retention-analysis.md#additional-retention-literature-policy-options-and-evidence-strength) records all 17 items and their limits.

## How the course economics changes the decision

The six supplied scans of Frank et al.'s *Principles of Economics* (4th ed., 2022) sharpen the decision rule. Opportunity cost and thinking at the margin (pp. 5–8) mean comparing the next Navy agreement with the best feasible civilian path, counting only benefits and costs that change. The inflation and saving chapters (pp. 324, 407–414) support constant 2026 dollars and a real discount rate for future pay and pension. The elasticity and labor-market chapters (pp. 73–80, 376–385) explain why pay might affect retention, but no textbook curve or salary gap estimates the Navy physician response. The [course crosswalk](../../capabilities/economic-research/course-economics-crosswalk.md) records the six-scan mapping, including less relevant macro and trade chapters.

BUS 620 Session 1’s elasticity lesson (pp. 18, 24–30) requires an observed change in retention after a compensation change. The oil responses in that lecture cannot stand in for a physician response. Micro C6’s consumer-choice and indifference-curve lessons (consumer choice pp. 5–14; reading pp. 3–9) show that cash, assignment stability, clinical practice, and family time form a bundle. Equal gross cash does not prove indifference between jobs.

Micro C4’s marginal-cost and sunk-cost distinction (pp. 7–15) means a future policy analysis should count new spending and avoidable future costs, not HPSP or training money already spent. The “Who Paid?” lecture (BUS 620 Session 3, pp. 35–43) calls for separating Navy fiscal expense, physician compensation, and any measured patient-access or readiness gain. The current table cannot establish a cost-effective new bonus.

## Test and objection

The obvious objection is that advertising cannot make up for a large salary gap or a harmful assignment. That may be right for many physicians. I would first verify that each advertised opportunity is available to the intended group, then compare targeted communication with standard information. Record whether a physician actually gets the promised mentoring, clinical work, or assignment option. Where feasible, compare communication with an operational improvement in assignment predictability or clinical access; retain financial changes as a separate policy option rather than assuming pay is unimportant. Measure accurate awareness, acceptance at an unobligated decision, and retention after the agreement. Analyze results by estimated financial gap, specialty, rank, and facility, while accounting for the existing FY26 internal-medicine pay change. Define the same post-obligation follow-up period for all groups, because accepting a longer contract is not itself durable retention. A change in stated intent alone would not establish a retention effect.

## What would change my answer

I would reject or narrow the communication hypothesis if accurate awareness improved but agreement acceptance and later retention did not, or if the advertised opportunities were not accessible in practice. If an actual assignment or clinical-practice improvement changed retention while communication alone did not, I would recommend the operational change. A weak effect among physicians with the largest verified gaps would also set a practical limit. I would revise the financial estimate if comparable local offers, taxes, benefits, or physician-paid expenses materially changed it. The current table does not measure the effect of communication or the value physicians place on nonfinancial attributes.

## Implementation and compensation caveat

FY26 [NAVADMIN 229/25](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/NAVADMIN%20229-25.pdf?ver=TfDkIMOG-xZUcffS8qWIAw%3D%3D) continues existing special-pay authority and directs officers to [Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Special-Pays/). This memo models current FY26 rates for an eligible new four-year agreement. An individual’s existing agreement may carry another rate; verify contract vintage and eligibility before using the estimate.

The opportunity-cost estimate needs a total-compensation check before a dollar schedule is set. Navy cash already includes BAH and BAS, which [DoD identifies as generally tax-exempt](https://militarypay.defense.gov/Pay/Tax-Information/Exempt/). An individual tax calculation could narrow the after-tax gap. Pension and BRS/TSP, TRICARE, and potential GI Bill transfer could add value **only to the extent staying changes what the physician receives**; already earned rights should not be credited again. Leave, clinical development, assignment stability, and mission value should be recorded alongside pay. A civilian offer may include its own retirement, health, leave, and malpractice benefits. I would use verified offers and physician-specific benefit inputs to estimate a range before deciding whether any policy change is warranted.

For evaluation, I would seek the annual provider-retention survey required by [Section 718 of Public Law 118-159](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-118publ159/pdf/PLAW-118publ159.pdf), alongside administrative records of bonus eligibility, acceptance, and eventual separation. The survey can reveal stated reasons for staying or leaving; the administrative records are needed to test whether the bonus changes behavior.

**Source note.** Preliminary internal survey findings signal retention concern, but they measure stated intentions rather than observed separations. Because the underlying briefing is controlled and has not been verified as publicly releasable, this public memo omits its figures. Obtain an approved aggregate release and match it to administrative retention outcomes before using those numbers as evidence in the paper.

**Pay-plan baseline.** The corrected [FY25 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY25/FY25%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE-Correction.pdf?ver=Fu3zLzlHCUzEu0ynrllaAQ%3D%3D) set the four-year general internal-medicine RB at $40,000 annually; [FY26 guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) raised it to $48,000. Pediatrics remained $35,000 and family medicine $48,000. The modeled baseline is therefore a new FY26-rate agreement, not a claim about every physician's existing contract. The evaluation should record agreement start year and actual RB/IP payments, and distinguish any effect of this existing $8,000 internal-medicine increase from concurrent changes.

**Next sensitivity.** [FY26 Medical Corps guidance](https://www.med.navy.mil/Portals/62/Documents/BUMED/Special%20Pays/FY26/FY26%20MC%20SPECIAL%20PAY%20GUIDANCE.pdf?ver=rucne9MfWLwappLSef4R3w%3D%3D) also lists six-year bonuses above the four-year schedule for these specialties. That current six-year agreement option should be compared only across a full six-year horizon, including the extra two years of obligation and the post-agreement retention outcome. For individual pension value, I would distinguish 20-year eligibility from student or training time credited in the retired-pay calculation, as described in the [DoD USU credit memorandum](https://mccareer.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/usuhs-retirement-credit.pdf), and verify the member's Statement of Service. Neither refinement changes the current four-year gross-cash arithmetic.
