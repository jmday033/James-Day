The Financial Opportunity Cost of Continued Navy Service for
Primary-Care Physicians
James M. Day
September 19, 2026
BUS 620 - Micro and Macro Economics
Individual Research Paper - A Global Challenge, Analyzed

<!-- PDF page 2 -->

Purpose and Research Question
The U.S. Navy Medical Corps must understand the financial alternatives physicians face at retention decisions.
Using a board-certified general internist in San Diego as the primary example, this paper asks: How much financial
compensation does a physician forgo by remaining in the Navy for the next four years rather than accepting a
feasible civilian position? The analysis informs, but does not determine, policy choices involving retention bonuses,
communication of military career benefits, working conditions, and staffing models.
The issue is economically important because recruiting a physician does not ensure retention after the initial
service obligation. The Government Accountability Office (GAO, 2020) found that maximum military cash
compensation was below the civilian median in 21 of 27 medical and dental specialties it reviewed and recommended
better civilian-wage, bonus-acceptance, and replacement-cost data. Those department-wide historical findings
motivate this case study, but they do not prove a current Navy primary-care pay gap.
Economic Framework
Opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative. Here, the financial opportunity cost of staying is the
present value of civilian cash compensation minus Navy cash compensation over the same four years. The comparison
is marginal: prior medical-school support is sunk, while future pay, additional obligations, retirement accrual, and
working conditions remain relevant (Frank et al., 2022). A civilian comparator is valid only if the physician is free to
separate, qualified, and reasonably able to obtain the position.
Cash compensation is only one part of the decision. Mission, camaraderie, teaching, leadership, operational
medicine, assignment control, workload, and family stability can create compensating wage differentials. Pension,
health coverage, tax advantages, and employer retirement contributions are financial benefits and should eventually be
valued as such. The cash gap therefore measures neither total career value nor the bonus necessary to retain a
physician.
Method and Assumptions
The base case is an O-4 general internist just past 10 years of active service, with dependents, assigned to ZIP
code 92134. The horizon is 2027-2030 in constant 2026 dollars. Rank remains O-4; basic pay advances from the
over-10 rate in years 1-2 to the over-12 rate in years 3-4. A 5% real discount rate and year-end payments are used. The
civilian benchmark is Marit Health's published San Diego internist average of $459,057, held constant in real terms.
Because it is a mixed-employer, self-reported average rather than a matched offer, results are conditional on its
comparability (Marit Health, 2026).

Pension Eligibility and the Missing Dynamic Incentive
The base case begins at 10 years and ends at 14, before the usual 20-year active-duty retirement threshold. No
pension payment is therefore included in the four-year cash table. This is a major limitation: the model does not
clearly quantify how the incentive to remain increases as a physician approaches 20 years. Leaving at 10 years means
giving up a distant and uncertain pension; leaving at 18 or 19 years means forfeiting a valuable lifetime benefit after
only one or two more years of service. The opportunity cost of separation therefore rises nonlinearly as eligibility
approaches - the familiar 'golden handcuffs' effect.
A simplified sensitivity illustrates the mechanism. Assuming a $120,000 High-3 basic-pay base, 30 years of
constant real pension payments, and a 5% discount rate, a BRS pension of $48,000 annually has a conditional present
value of about $453,000 at 10 years, $669,000 at 18 years, and $703,000 at 19 years. These are not amounts to
subtract directly from the four-year cash gap: they assume the member reaches 20 years and omit foregone civilian
earnings, taxes, uncertainty, and alternative Reserve benefits. They demonstrate why career stage must be modeled
explicitly (Department of Defense, n.d.).
Moonlighting also requires a separate scenario because it adds labor and may be limited by deployments,
reassignment, approval, fatigue, and malpractice expenses. Educational benefits already received under HPSP, HSCP,
or USUHS are sunk and should not be counted again as new retention compensation.

The base case produces a four-year nominal cash gap of $717,126 and a present-value gap of approximately
$636,209. The over-12 longevity increase narrows the annual gap beginning in 2029 but does not reverse it. At a 3%
real discount rate, the gap is $666,715; at 7%, it is $607,907. A $10,000 change in annual civilian compensation
changes the four-year present value by about $35,460. If the assumed $48,000 annual retention agreement is
unavailable, the four-year present-value gap increases by about $170,206.
These results are accounting estimates, not predictions of separation. They do not show how strongly physicians
respond to pay, the value physicians place on noncash job attributes, or the cost to the Navy of replacing a departing
physician. The mixed-employer benchmark may overstate or understate the best feasible alternative for a particular
officer. A matched offer would need to account for workload, call, experience, practice setting, location, and
employer-funded benefits.
Policy Options
The initial recommendation is a limited, testable communication campaign aimed at physicians approaching
retention decisions. Existing channels could explain real opportunities for mission-focused practice, leadership,
teaching, research, and operational medicine while separately clarifying special-pay eligibility, tax treatment, and
retirement consequences. Communication may correct an information gap, but it cannot close the cash gap or make
inaccessible opportunities available. The campaign should therefore be candid about deployments, geographic
mobility, administrative burden, and assignment uncertainty.
Success should be measured through baseline awareness, access to advertised opportunities, agreement
acceptance, and later retention compared with standard communication. Clicks and views are not retention outcomes.
If awareness increases without changes in acceptance or retention, the Navy should redirect attention toward
compensation or working conditions. Army research suggests that family well-being, deployment effects, workload,
and workplace factors matter alongside pay, although those findings should not be treated as Navy-specific causal
estimates (Vie et al., 2024; Wojcik et al., 2020).
Bonus advocacy is a complementary option. A larger bonus directly changes financial compensation, but the cash
gap alone cannot identify the optimal amount. Evaluation must estimate the marginal cost per additional physician
retained, including payments to physicians who would have stayed without the increase. Agreement uptake must also
be separated from genuinely additional service obtained.

Policy Options, Continued
A third option is to shift appropriate primary-care work toward nurse practitioners and physician assistants while
preserving physician capacity for complex care, teaching, supervision, and operational readiness. VA studies
demonstrate that advanced practice clinicians can manage substantial primary-care workloads and, in selected
populations, may be associated with similar measured outcomes and lower utilization or costs (Liu et al., 2020;
Morgan et al., 2019). These findings do not establish one-for-one substitution or a Navy savings rate. A Navy pilot
should compare total team costs, access, quality, downstream utilization, workload, and readiness under different
staffing configurations.
Working-condition reforms may be equally important. Military and civilian studies link burnout and
turnover-related outcomes with administrative burden, workload, schedule control, leadership support, and work-life
concerns (Agarwal et al., 2020; Korona-Bailey et al., 2024; Wilk et al., 2023). Advertising attractive missions without
addressing these barriers may undermine credibility. Compensation, job design, and communication should therefore
be evaluated as related but distinct interventions.
Limitations
The analysis uses one O-4 profile, one location, and one civilian benchmark. It assumes continued eligibility for a
four-year retention agreement, holds compensation constant in real terms, and excludes promotion. It also omits taxes,
health coverage, employer retirement contributions, malpractice and transition costs, and the probability-adjusted
value of future retirement benefits. These omissions may narrow or widen the measured gap.
Most importantly, the fixed four-year horizon masks the changing incentive created by pension proximity. The
cash model should not be interpreted as though a physician at 10 years faces the same stay-or-leave decision as one at
18 or 19 years. Future work should model career-stage scenarios through retirement eligibility, apply the correct
High-3 or BRS rules, distinguish vested TSP balances from the defined-benefit pension, and compare active-duty,
Reserve, and civilian paths using consistent timing and discounting.
The model also does not measure actual Navy primary-care attrition, causal readiness losses, retention elasticities,
or replacement cost. Before using the estimate for policy advocacy, the Medical Corps should validate individual pay
entitlements and comparable civilian offers by specialty and career stage. Any intervention should track eligible
populations, costs, additional service obtained, working conditions, and actual retention using a credible comparison
group.
Conclusion
For the modeled O-4 general internist, remaining in the Navy rather than taking the published San Diego
benchmark produces a four-year gross-cash opportunity cost of approximately $636,209 in present value at 5%. The
estimate is substantial but incomplete. It excludes tax and benefit differences and does not fully capture the rapidly
increasing value of staying as pension eligibility approaches.
The best near-term response is not to assume that one intervention solves retention. The Navy should test
accurate, targeted communication as a low-cost first step; evaluate larger bonuses using marginal cost per additional
physician retained; improve working conditions where operationally feasible; and pilot team-based staffing for
appropriate clinical work. Better specialty- and career-stage data are necessary to determine which combination
produces the greatest retention and readiness value.
