The Financial Opportunity Cost of Continued Navy Service
for Primary-Care Physicians
James M. Day
September 19, 2026
BUS 620 - Micro and Macro Economics
Individual Research Paper - A Global Challenge, Analyzed

<!-- PDF page 2 -->

Purpose and Economic Framework
The U.S. Navy Medical Corps must understand the alternatives physicians face when
deciding whether to remain after their initial obligation. Using a board-certified general
internist in San Diego, this paper asks: How much financial compensation does a physician
forgo by remaining in the Navy for four more years rather than accepting a feasible
civilian position? GAO (2020) found that maximum military cash compensation trailed
civilian medians in 21 of 27 medical and dental specialties reviewed and recommended better
wage, bonus, and replacement-cost data. Those findings motivate this case study but do not
prove a current Navy primary-care pay gap.
Opportunity cost is the value of the best forgone alternative. Here it is measured as the
present value of civilian cash compensation minus Navy cash compensation over the same
period. Prior medical-school support is sunk; future pay, obligations, retirement accrual, and
working conditions remain relevant (Frank et al., 2022). Mission, leadership, teaching,
operational medicine, assignment control, workload, and family stability may also create
compensating wage differentials. Therefore, the cash gap is not a complete valuation of either
career and does not identify the bonus required for retention.
Method
The base case is an O-4 general internist just past 10 active-service years, with
dependents, assigned to ZIP code 92134. The horizon is 2027-2030 in constant 2026 dollars.
Rank remains O-4; basic pay advances from the over-10 to the over-12 rate. The analysis uses
a 5% real discount rate and year-end payments. The civilian comparator is Marit Health's
published San Diego internist average of $459,057, held constant in real terms (Marit Health,
2026). Because this is a mixed-employer, self-reported benchmark rather than a matched offer,
the result remains conditional on its comparability.

t
Each annual gap is discounted as (civilian cash - Navy cash)/(1.05). BAH and BAS
receive federal income-tax exclusions, so equal gross cash would not imply equal take-home
pay (Internal Revenue Service, 2025). A full comparison would also include taxes, health
coverage, employer retirement contributions, malpractice costs, and transition expenses.
Pension Limitation
The base case ends at 14 years, before the usual 20-year active-duty retirement
threshold, so no pension payment appears in the four-year cash table. This omits an important
career-stage effect. Conditional on completing service to 20 years, the present value of the
modeled pension increases as eligibility approaches; overall incentives also depend on
civilian earnings and other circumstances. For illustration, assuming a $120,000 High-3
basic-pay base, 30 constant real payments, and a 5% discount rate, a $48,000 BRS pension has
a conditional present value of about $453,000 at 10 years, $669,000 at 18 years, and $703,000
at 19 years. These values cannot simply be subtracted from the four-year cash gap because the
horizons and assumptions differ (Department of Defense, n.d.).

Results
Figure 1. Annual Navy and published local benchmark cash compensation, with the cumulative discounted gap. The civilian
series is not a matched offer. Pension and other benefits are excluded.
The base case produces an undiscounted cash gap in constant 2026 dollars of
$717,126 and a present-value gap of approximately $636,209. At 3%, the present-value gap is
$666,715; at 7%, it is $607,907. Without the assumed $48,000 retention agreement, the
four-year present-value gap rises by approximately $170,206. These estimates compare
compensation streams; they do not predict separation.

Policy Implications
The Navy should test a limited communication pilot that explains special pays,
retirement consequences, and accessible career opportunities. Communication may correct an
information gap but cannot close the cash gap. Larger bonuses directly change compensation,
yet the model does not identify an optimal amount. Evaluation should measure agreement
acceptance, actual retention, and marginal cost per additional physician retained.
Additional Options
Team-based staffing could shift appropriate work toward nurse practitioners and
physician assistants while preserving physician capacity for complex care and readiness. VA
evidence supports feasibility in selected populations but not one-for-one Navy substitution
(Liu et al., 2020; Morgan et al., 2019). The Navy should also address workload, administrative
burden, leadership support, deployment, and work-life concerns associated with burnout and
turnover-related outcomes (Vie et al., 2024; Wilk et al., 2023).
Limitations
The model uses one rank, location, and civilian benchmark. It assumes bonus eligibility
and excludes promotion, taxes, health coverage, retirement contributions, malpractice costs,
moonlighting constraints, and probability-adjusted pension value. It does not estimate Navy
attrition, replacement costs, retention elasticity, or readiness losses. Future analysis should
compare matched career paths by specialty and service stage.
Conclusion
For the modeled O-4 internist, the four-year present-value gross-cash gap is
approximately $636,209 at 5%. The estimate is substantial but incomplete. Conditional on
completing service to 20 years, the present value of the modeled pension increases as
eligibility approaches; overall incentives also depend on civilian earnings and other
circumstances. The Navy should test targeted communication, evaluate bonuses by marginal
retention effect, improve working conditions, and collect better career-stage data.
