Hereditary Hemochromatosis Identified During Aviation Medical Evaluation of an Unmanned Aircraft Pilot

(Alternative Title: Early Detection of Hereditary Hemochromatosis During Long-Form Aviation Physical: Implications for Aerospace Medicine)

Abstract

Background: Hereditary hemochromatosis (HH) is an inherited disorder of iron metabolism characterized by excessive intestinal iron absorption and progressive iron accumulation, with potential hepatic, cardiac, endocrine, and musculoskeletal complications. Early identification and treatment can prevent or reduce the development of irreversible organ injury. Aviation medical evaluations provide an opportunity to identify occult disease in otherwise healthy personnel, particularly when targeted screening is prompted by relevant family history.

Case: A 30-year-old male unmanned aircraft pilot presented for a long-form aviation medical examination. He reported a maternal family history of hereditary hemochromatosis and noted intermittent joint pain and fatigue but had an otherwise normal review of systems and physical examination. Initial laboratory evaluation was largely unremarkable, including a normal complete blood count and comprehensive metabolic panel, with the exception of an alanine aminotransferase of 57 U/L. Given his family history, additional iron studies were obtained and demonstrated elevated serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, transferrin saturation, and ferritin. Subsequent HFE genetic testing demonstrated C282Y homozygosity. His transferrin saturation was 96% and serum ferritin was 840 ng/mL. Gastrointestinal and Hematology/Oncology consultations were obtained. Magnetic resonance imaging of the liver demonstrated moderate hepatic iron accumulation, with an estimated liver iron concentration of 8.46 mg/g. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal cardiac structure and function with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 60–65%. There was no clinical evidence of heart failure, hypogonadism, or diabetes. Therapeutic phlebotomy every two weeks was recommended, with a target ferritin of 50–150 ng/mL and treatment held for hemoglobin below 10 g/dL.

Conclusion: This case demonstrates the value of incorporating family history and targeted laboratory testing into aviation medical evaluations. In this otherwise healthy young unmanned aircraft pilot, recognition of hereditary hemochromatosis allowed identification of substantial iron overload before clinically apparent cardiac, endocrine, or advanced hepatic complications developed. The case highlights the role of aerospace medicine in early disease detection, longitudinal risk assessment, and preservation of operational health.

Introduction

Hereditary hemochromatosis is an inherited disorder characterized by dysregulated iron absorption and progressive iron accumulation in tissues. There are variants in five genes that include HFE, HJV, HAMP, TFR2, and SLC40A1. However the most common cases of hereditary hemochromatosis is HFE-related disease, with homozygosity for the p.Cys282Tyr (C282Y) variant representing the classic genotype associated with clinically significant iron loading. However, genetic penetrance is variable, and individuals with C282Y homozygosity may remain asymptomatic for years despite progressive biochemical iron accumulation.

Clinical manifestations of iron overload generally take several decades, which may include fatigue, arthralgia, liver disease, diabetes mellitus, hypogonadism, cardiomyopathy, and other end-organ complications. Importantly, symptoms may be nonspecific or absent during earlier stages of disease. Current guidelines therefore emphasize evaluation of transferrin saturation and ferritin in individuals with suggestive symptoms, evidence of organ involvement, or a family history of hereditary hemochromatosis.

Early diagnosis is clinically important because therapeutic phlebotomy can reduce iron stores and prevent progression to severe complications. The European Association for the Study of the Liver recommends assessment for hepatic and other end-organ involvement at diagnosis and emphasizes early treatment before irreversible complications develop.

From an aerospace medicine perspective, identification of occult medical conditions is particularly relevant when disease or its treatment could eventually affect fatigue, physical performance, cognition, or the ability to safely perform operational duties. This case describes the identification of HFE-related hereditary hemochromatosis during a long-form aviation medical evaluation of a young unmanned aircraft pilot. The case illustrates how targeted evaluation prompted by family history can identify clinically significant iron overload before the development of major end-organ disease.

Case Presentation

A 30-year-old male unmanned aircraft pilot presented for a long-form aviation medical examination. He was generally healthy and had no known significant chronic medical conditions. His family history was notable for hereditary hemochromatosis on his maternal side.

During the clinical interview, the patient reported intermittent joint pain and fatigue. He otherwise denied symptoms suggestive of significant end-organ dysfunction. In particular, there were no symptoms concerning for heart failure, hypogonadism, or diabetes mellitus. Physical examination was normal without notable abnormalities.

Initial laboratory testing demonstrated a normal complete blood count and otherwise largely normal comprehensive metabolic panel. The primary abnormality was a mildly elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 57 U/L. Given the patient's family history of hereditary hemochromatosis and nonspecific symptoms of fatigue and joint discomfort, additional iron studies were obtained.

Further testing demonstrated elevations in serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, transferrin saturation, and ferritin. Given the combination of abnormal iron studies and a positive maternal family history, internal medicine was consulted for further evaluation. HFE genetic testing was subsequently performed and demonstrated homozygosity for the C282Y variant.

The patient was subsequently found to have a transferrin saturation of 96% and serum ferritin of 840 ng/mL. These findings were consistent with substantial iron overload in the setting of C282Y homozygosity. In C282Y homozygotes, elevated transferrin saturation and ferritin are sufficient to establish a diagnosis of HFE-related hemochromatosis in the appropriate clinical context.

Hematology/Oncology recommended therapeutic phlebotomy every two weeks with a goal of reducing serum ferritin to 50–150 ng/mL. Hemoglobin was to be monitored during treatment, with phlebotomy held if hemoglobin decreased below 10 g/dL.

Because of the degree of iron overload, evaluation for end-organ involvement was pursued. The patient had no clinical manifestations concerning for heart failure, hypogonadism, or diabetes. His AST remained within normal limits, while his ALT was near the upper end of the normal range following the initial mild elevation.

Gastroenterology was consulted, and magnetic resonance imaging of the liver was obtained for noninvasive assessment of hepatic iron burden. MRI findings were compatible with moderate elevation of hepatic iron concentration, with an estimated liver iron concentration of 8.46 mg/g.

Cardiac evaluation was performed with transthoracic echocardiography. The study demonstrated normal cardiac structure and function without sonographic evidence of significant functional, structural, or valvular heart disease. Left ventricular systolic function was preserved, with an estimated ejection fraction of 60–65%. The cardiac valves were structurally and functionally normal without clinically significant stenosis or regurgitation.

At the time of diagnosis, therefore, the patient had C282Y homozygous HFE-related hereditary hemochromatosis with marked biochemical iron overload and moderate hepatic iron accumulation, but without identified clinically significant cardiac, endocrine, or advanced symptomatic organ complications.

Discussion

Hereditary hemochromatosis represents a clinically important but potentially treatable cause of systemic iron overload. In HFE-related disease, the C282Y variant disrupts normal regulation of iron homeostasis, resulting in increased intestinal iron absorption and progressive iron accumulation. C282Y homozygosity is the predominant genotype associated with classic HFE-related hereditary hemochromatosis.

The clinical presentation of this patient illustrates several important features of the disease. First, the patient was relatively young and had a normal physical examination despite substantial biochemical iron overload. His only reported symptoms were fatigue and possible joint pain, both of which are nonspecific. Fatigue and arthralgia are recognized manifestations of hereditary hemochromatosis, but neither symptom alone would necessarily lead to a diagnosis.

Second, the patient's maternal family history was critical to the diagnostic evaluation. Current recommendations support obtaining transferrin saturation and ferritin in individuals with a family history of hereditary hemochromatosis. If either is abnormal, HFE genetic testing is appropriate. First-degree relatives of individuals with confirmed hereditary hemochromatosis are also candidates for screening.

In this patient, the combination of C282Y homozygosity, transferrin saturation of 96%, and ferritin of 840 ng/mL provided strong evidence of clinically significant iron overload. The European Association for the Study of the Liver identifies transferrin saturation above 50% and ferritin above 300 μg/L in men with C282Y homozygosity as sufficient evidence of provisional iron overload in the appropriate clinical setting.

The patient's hepatic MRI further demonstrated the physiological consequence of the genetic and biochemical findings. MRI-based hepatic iron quantification provides a noninvasive method of assessing hepatic iron accumulation and can be particularly useful in evaluating the extent of iron overload.

The absence of major end-organ complications in this patient is clinically significant. Untreated hereditary hemochromatosis can progress to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, hypogonadism, and arthropathy. Early identification and treatment are therefore important even when the patient is relatively asymptomatic.

Therapeutic phlebotomy remains the primary treatment for HFE-related hereditary hemochromatosis. The objective is to progressively reduce body iron stores while avoiding treatment-associated anemia. Contemporary European guidelines generally recommend a ferritin target below 50 μg/L during the induction phase and below 100 μg/L during maintenance therapy, while the treating Hematology/Oncology team in this case selected a target range of 50–150 ng/mL.

The patient's ferritin of 840 ng/mL was below the 1,000 ng/mL threshold that has historically been associated with increased concern for advanced hepatic fibrosis in C282Y homozygotes, although liver assessment remains important because individual risk varies. His normal platelet count and relatively limited transaminase abnormality, combined with MRI assessment, provided additional context for evaluating hepatic involvement.

The cardiac evaluation was also particularly relevant in this case. Iron deposition can cause cardiomyopathy and other cardiac manifestations in advanced iron overload. The patient's normal echocardiogram and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 60–65% provided reassuring evidence that clinically significant structural or systolic cardiac disease had not developed at the time of evaluation.

The case therefore represents an opportunity for early intervention: significant iron accumulation was detected while major cardiac, endocrine, and advanced hepatic manifestations remained absent. This is an important distinction because treatment initiated before the development of irreversible organ damage has the potential to prevent future morbidity.

Aerospace Medicine Significance

The aerospace medicine significance of this case extends beyond the diagnosis of hereditary hemochromatosis itself. The condition was identified during a long-form aviation medical evaluation in an otherwise healthy 30-year-old unmanned aircraft pilot, rather than after presentation with overt end-organ disease.

Aviation medicine places particular emphasis on identifying conditions that could compromise operational performance, either through sudden incapacitation or through more subtle effects on physical or cognitive performance. Although this patient did not demonstrate clinically significant cardiac or endocrine complications, his reported fatigue and joint symptoms were potentially relevant from an operational perspective.

This is particularly pertinent in unmanned aircraft operations. Although the operator is not physically occupying the aircraft, UAS operations remain dependent on human performance, decision-making, sustained attention, and appropriate management of fatigue. The FAA Office of Aerospace Medicine has specifically identified fatigue and fitness for duty as important considerations in UAS operations and has noted the need for further understanding of physiological and human-performance factors affecting UAS operators.

The case also highlights the importance of family history as an aerospace medical screening tool. Routine examination alone would not necessarily have identified this patient's condition. His normal examination and largely normal initial laboratory evaluation could have resulted in no further investigation had the maternal family history not prompted targeted iron studies.

The discovery of hereditary hemochromatosis also permits longitudinal assessment of factors that may become operationally relevant. These include:

- Development of hepatic dysfunction or advanced fibrosis

- Cardiac involvement

- Endocrine complications such as hypogonadism or diabetes

- Progression of arthropathy

- Fatigue and exercise tolerance

- Hemoglobin response during therapeutic phlebotomy

- Potential effects of treatment on operational duties

From an aviation medical standpoint, the patient's normal echocardiogram and absence of clinically apparent cardiac, endocrine, or advanced hepatic disease provide an important baseline for future monitoring. His hepatic MRI similarly establishes objective evidence of the current degree of iron accumulation.

The case additionally demonstrates the value of multidisciplinary management in an aviation population. Internal Medicine, Hematology/Oncology, Gastroenterology, and aerospace medicine each contributed to different aspects of diagnosis, assessment, treatment, and operational risk evaluation.

For aviation medical providers, this case reinforces that a long-form examination should not be viewed solely as a checklist-based assessment. A detailed family history can identify individuals who warrant targeted testing despite minimal symptoms. In this patient, recognition of an inherited predisposition led to identification of substantial iron overload and allowed treatment to begin before significant clinical complications developed.

Current FAA guidance specifically lists primary hemochromatosis among conditions of aeromedical significance and provides disposition guidance for aviation medical examiners.

Conclusion

This case describes a 30-year-old unmanned aircraft pilot in whom C282Y homozygous hereditary hemochromatosis was identified during a long-form aviation medical evaluation. Despite a normal physical examination and minimal nonspecific symptoms, targeted evaluation prompted by a maternal family history revealed marked iron overload, with a transferrin saturation of 96%, ferritin of 840 ng/mL, and MRI evidence of moderate hepatic iron accumulation.

Importantly, evaluation did not demonstrate clinically significant cardiac, endocrine, or advanced symptomatic hepatic complications at the time of diagnosis. Early recognition allowed initiation of therapeutic phlebotomy and establishment of a multidisciplinary follow-up plan.

From an aerospace medicine perspective, this case demonstrates the value of comprehensive history-taking and targeted screening in identifying occult disease in operational personnel. In an unmanned aircraft pilot, maintaining human performance and preventing progression to disease that could affect fatigue, physical function, cognition, or operational readiness is an important component of preventive aerospace medicine.

Ultimately, this case illustrates how an aviation medical evaluation can serve not only as an assessment of current fitness but also as an opportunity for early detection, prevention of disease progression, and preservation of long-term operational health.
